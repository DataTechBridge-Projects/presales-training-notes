# Where AI Creates Value: Automation, Augmentation & Differentiation
{: .no_toc }

*Read duration: 4:29*

Not every AI opportunity creates value the same way, and conflating the three ways it does is one of the fastest routes to a proposal that overpromises. A simple three-part framework keeps this straight in front of a client: **automation**, **augmentation**, and **differentiation**.

## Automation: Doing Existing Work Cheaper or Faster

**Automation** means AI does something a person currently does, at lower cost or higher speed, without changing what gets produced. The value story is efficiency — hours saved, cost per unit of work reduced. A pre-sales-relevant example is the proposal drafting use case from earlier in this section: an AI tool producing a first-draft executive summary from discovery notes doesn't change what a proposal is or does, it just gets a human to a usable draft faster than starting from a blank page. Automation is usually the easiest AI value to sell, because it's the easiest to measure — you can compare hours before and after directly.

## Augmentation: Making People Better at Existing Work

**Augmentation** means AI doesn't replace a step, it makes the person doing that step sharper — surfacing something they'd have missed, or giving them a second perspective before they decide. The value story is quality and judgment, not raw speed. The estimation use case from earlier in this section fits here: an AI tool flagging that a scope draft is missing a deliverable a similar past SOW included doesn't write the estimate for you, it makes the estimator's judgment better informed before they finalize a number a client will hold them to. The human is still fully in control of the decision; the AI just widened what they could see before making it.

## Differentiation: Enabling Something Not Possible Before

**Differentiation** is the category with the highest ceiling and the least certainty — AI doesn't just do existing work faster or better, it makes something possible that genuinely wasn't before, at least not at a cost or speed that made sense to offer. The clickable-prototype technique covered earlier in this section is a clean example: showing a buyer an interactive, click-through mockup of their own workflow during an RFP response wasn't a faster version of something pre-sales teams already did — building a real interactive prototype in a day, cheaply enough to do it for a deal that might not close, simply wasn't something most teams could justify before. It's a genuinely new capability in the sales cycle, not an accelerated old one.

## Using the Framework With a Client

| Category | Question to ask | Value story | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Automation | What are we doing today that AI could do at the same quality, faster or cheaper? | Cost and time savings | AI-drafted first-pass proposal sections |
| Augmentation | Where do people make judgment calls that AI could make sharper without taking over? | Better decisions, same person in control | AI flagging a missing SOW deliverable during estimation |
| Differentiation | What could we offer that wasn't feasible or affordable to offer before? | New competitive capability | A clickable prototype built during an RFP response |

These three categories aren't just a teaching tool — they map directly onto how you should pitch AI value to a client. Automation is the easiest to cost-justify with a straightforward before-and-after comparison. Augmentation is a harder sell numerically but often the safest starting point, since a human stays accountable for the final call. Differentiation is the hardest to price up front and the easiest to overpromise, precisely because there's no "before" state to compare it to — which is exactly why the cost-and-risk framework later in this section exists, to keep differentiation pitches grounded instead of speculative.
