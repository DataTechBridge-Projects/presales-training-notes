# Business Trip Planning
{: .no_toc }

*Read duration: 6:02*

A well-run business trip can compress weeks of email back-and-forth into a single afternoon of real conversation. A poorly run one burns a travel budget, tires out your account team, and leaves the client wondering why you bothered showing up. The difference almost never comes down to the flight or the hotel — it comes down to preparation before you leave, discipline while you're on-site, and speed once you're back.

## Before You Go

The single biggest predictor of a good trip is **agenda alignment** with the account team days in advance, not the morning of. Before you book anything, confirm three things with the account executive or customer success owner: who from the client side will actually be in the room, what decision or milestone this trip is meant to advance, and what "success" looks like by the time you fly home. A trip with no defined outcome — "let's just go meet them" — tends to produce a pleasant lunch and nothing else.

Once the agenda is set, confirm **stakeholders** by name and role, not just by title. "We're meeting with IT" is not the same as knowing you'll be in a room with the infrastructure lead who controls the budget and the security architect who will veto anything that fails their checklist. Ask the account team to send you a short brief on each attendee: their role, their stake in the outcome, and any history — good or bad — with your company. Walking in blind to a room that already has an opinion about your product is how deals quietly die.

Pack for failure, not just for success. Bring your **demo environment** on a laptop that doesn't depend on the client's Wi-Fi, and have a recorded backup — screenshots or a short video walkthrough — in case live software simply won't cooperate in an unfamiliar network or a locked-down corporate guest network. Print the proposal, the SOW draft, or the one-pager even if you've already emailed it; paper doesn't run out of battery, and handing someone a physical document in a meeting still carries weight. If the trip is international, spend twenty minutes on local business customs before you go — how meetings typically open, whether business cards are exchanged formally, appropriate dress, and even how directly you can raise pricing in the first meeting. Getting this wrong doesn't sink a deal by itself, but it's an unforced error that's entirely avoidable with a little homework.

{: .important }
> Always carry an offline fallback for your demo. A live environment that depends on the client's network is a single point of failure you don't control — and losing the room to a spinning loading screen is one of the most avoidable ways to waste a trip.

## While You're There

Time zones aren't just a jet-lag problem — they shape how sharp you'll be in the room. If you're flying across more than two or three zones, build in a buffer day rather than landing the night before a morning executive briefing. Clients notice when the person across the table is visibly running on fumes, and it undercuts the confidence you're trying to project.

The formal agenda is only part of the value of being on-site. Some of the best **informal discovery** happens in the hallway before the meeting starts, over the coffee machine, or during the walk from reception to the conference room. Casual comments — "we've been burned by a vendor before," "our board is watching this initiative closely" — rarely surface in a scheduled discovery call but come out naturally in person. Treat every interaction with the client, not just the scheduled ones, as part of the conversation.

Reading the room matters more in person than on any call. Watch who defers to whom, who's checking their phone, and who leans in when you mention a specific capability — that's often your real champion revealing themselves. If the room's energy shifts when pricing comes up or a specific competitor is mentioned, don't plow through your slides; pause and ask about it directly. An on-site meeting gives you signal density a video call never will, and it's wasted if you're heads-down in your deck instead of watching the people in front of you.

## After You Leave

The value of a trip decays fast. Send a follow-up — a short recap of what was discussed, the agreed next steps, and any commitments made on both sides — within 24 hours, ideally the same evening if the meeting ended early enough. Loop in the account team immediately with a debrief on what you observed: the real decision-maker, any objections that surfaced informally, and anything you noticed on-site that didn't make it into the presentation itself. That debrief often matters more to the deal than the meeting notes, because it captures the judgment calls a transcript can't.
