# Pricing Models (T&M, Fixed, Retainer, Outcome-Based)
{: .no_toc }

*Read duration: 1:41*

The pricing model you choose shifts risk between you and the client as much as it sets a number, and the wrong one for the situation causes more friction than the wrong price. **Time & Materials (T&M)** bills actual hours at agreed rates — the most flexible model, suited to evolving scope, but most of the overrun risk sits with the client. **Fixed price** quotes a single number for a defined scope — predictable and easy for procurement to approve, but the estimation risk sits with you: a wrong WBS means you absorb the overage. A **retainer** sells a recurring block of capacity (say, 40 hours a month) rather than a project, suiting ongoing advisory or managed-service work where the *type* of task is known but the specifics aren't yet. **Outcome-based** pricing ties payment to a measurable result — a go-live, a defined efficiency gain — rather than hours or scope; it's compelling to a value-focused buyer, but only workable when you control enough of the variables to actually guarantee that outcome.

| Model | Predictability | Client risk | Vendor risk | Best-fit scenario |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| T&M | Low | High — cost can grow with scope | Low | Evolving or poorly-defined scope, discovery-heavy work |
| Fixed Price | High | Low | High — vendor absorbs overruns | Well-scoped work with a mature WBS and few unknowns |
| Retainer | Medium | Medium — pays for capacity, not guaranteed output | Low-medium | Ongoing advisory, managed services, unpredictable ticket volume |
| Outcome-Based | High (on cost, if outcome hits) | Low if outcome is achieved | High — vendor unpaid if outcome misses | Vendor controls the variables and the outcome is clearly measurable |

{: .important }
> Never propose fixed price against a scope you haven't broken down with a WBS — without that detail, "fixed" just means you've quietly agreed to absorb every estimation mistake yourself.

In practice, many proposals blend models — a fixed-price implementation phase followed by a T&M or retainer support phase — because the risk profile of the work genuinely changes between "building something new" and "keeping something running." Naming the model explicitly in the proposal, and tying it back to the scope and assumptions sections, is what keeps a client from later expecting fixed-price certainty from what was actually scoped as T&M.
