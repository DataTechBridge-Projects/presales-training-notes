# Scope, Deliverables, Assumptions, Risks
{: .no_toc }

*Read duration: 1:44*

Four short sections in a proposal do more to prevent disputes than any other part of the document, and they're the ones teams rush through because the pricing table feels more urgent. **Scope** is the boundary of the engagement — not just what's included, but explicitly what's excluded, because an unstated exclusion is one the client will assume is included. If you're proposing a CRM migration, "data migration" is meaningless until you state which objects, which date range of history, and which legacy fields are out of scope. **Deliverables** are the tangible, verifiable artifacts scope produces — a configured environment, a signed-off test plan, a training deck — each one specific enough that both sides can agree, without argument, that it's been delivered.

**Assumptions** are the conditions your estimate and price depend on being true, and they exist because you can't know everything at proposal time. A real example: "Client will provide a sandbox environment with production-like data volume by week 2" or "Existing API documentation is current and complete." If either assumption turns out false, your estimate is no longer valid — which is exactly the point of writing it down, since it gives you contractual grounds to reopen scope or price rather than silently absorbing the gap.

{: .important }
> An assumption you didn't write down becomes a commitment you didn't intend to make — if it's not in the proposal, the client will reasonably expect you to handle it within the original price.

**Risks** are different from assumptions: they're things that could go wrong even if your assumptions hold, like a third-party vendor missing an integration deadline or a client's internal approval process taking longer than planned. A short risk register — risk, likelihood, impact, mitigation — signals maturity to a sophisticated buyer, because it shows you've thought past the happy path. Naming a risk in the proposal, with a proposed mitigation, is also how you protect a change order later: if the risk materializes exactly as you described, you've already established that it sits outside the original scope.
