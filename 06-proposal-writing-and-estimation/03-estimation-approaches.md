# Estimation Approaches
{: .no_toc }

*Read duration: 2:57*

Estimation is where a proposal's credibility is won or lost, because a padded number gets you underbid and a thin number gets you underwater. There's no single correct method — the right approach depends on how much you actually know about the work, and mature pre-sales teams often blend more than one.

### Top-down methods

**Analogous estimation** compares the current opportunity to a similar past engagement — "our last mid-market CRM migration ran 400 hours, this one is roughly the same size, so we'll estimate close to that." It's fast and useful early, when you don't yet have enough detail to break the work down, but it's only as good as how genuinely comparable the past project was. **Parametric estimation** tightens this by using a measurable driver — cost or hours per data source migrated, per integration point, per user trained — so the estimate scales with a real unit of the client's specific scope rather than a gut-feel comparison.

### Bottom-up methods

A **work breakdown structure (WBS)** decomposes the engagement into discrete tasks — data mapping, environment setup, integration build, UAT, training — and estimates each one individually before summing them. It takes longer to build but produces a far more defensible number, because you can show your work if a client pushes back on the total. Within each task, **three-point estimation** (optimistic, most likely, pessimistic, often weighted as a PERT average) captures uncertainty explicitly rather than picking one number and hoping.

### Buffers and contingency

No estimate should go into a proposal without a **buffer** — a contingency percentage added on top to absorb the unknowns that inevitably surface once work starts. The size of the buffer should track the size of your assumptions and risks: a well-scoped, low-risk engagement might carry 10-15% contingency, while a project full of unknowns (unfamiliar legacy systems, an unclear data source) might justify 25-30%.

{: .important }
> Never present a bottom-up estimate as if it were exact — a WBS total plus a stated contingency is honest; a single "precise" number with no buffer just moves the risk from your estimate onto your delivery team.
