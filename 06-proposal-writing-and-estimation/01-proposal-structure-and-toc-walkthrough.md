# Proposal Structure & TOC Walkthrough
{: .no_toc }

*Read duration: 1:54*

A proposal is not a filing cabinet for everything you know about the deal — it's an argument, and the **table of contents (TOC)** is that argument's outline. Buyers, especially procurement or a selection committee, often skim the TOC before reading a single page, so its order silently tells them whether you understood their problem or you're just recycling a template. The single biggest structural mistake is opening with a company overview: "About Us" as page one signals you're talking about yourself before you've proven you listened. Lead instead with the client's problem, framed in their language from discovery, so the first thing a reader sees is proof you paid attention.

A proposal that tells a compelling story generally moves through a consistent sequence: an **executive summary** stating the problem and your recommended outcome in a few sentences; **understanding of requirements**, mirroring back what you heard in discovery; the **proposed approach**, the how; **scope and deliverables**, the what; a **timeline**; **team and roles**; **assumptions and risks**; **pricing**; and commercial **terms**, with supporting detail pushed to appendices. Each section should answer the question the previous one raises — understanding the problem earns the right to propose an approach, the approach justifies the scope, the scope justifies the price.

{: .important }
> Never lead a proposal with your company's history or capabilities. Lead with the client's problem, restated in their words — the "About Us" section belongs near the end, as supporting evidence, not the opening pitch.

Pricing should never appear before the reader understands what they're buying and why it's structured that way — a number without context invites the reader to negotiate against it in isolation rather than judge it against value. Keep the executive summary genuinely short (half a page, not three) since it's often the only section a senior stakeholder reads in full; everything after it exists to let a more technical evaluator verify the claims the summary makes.
