# Documenting and Tracking Scope Changes
{: .no_toc }

*Read duration: 2:21*

The **change order** itself — the contract document that formally amends scope — was covered in Core Contract Documents. This lecture is about the daily discipline that keeps that document honest: tracking every request as it happens, before it turns into a dispute.

Scope drifts in small, deniable pieces. A client asks for "one more field on the report" in a status call, someone says "sure, that's easy," and three months later there are a dozen such asides that add real hours nobody billed for or planned around. None of them felt big enough to write up at the time. That's exactly why you need a running **change log** — a simple shared record (a spreadsheet or tracker is fine) that captures every scope request the moment it's raised: date, who asked, what was requested, your initial read on impact, and its current status (logged, estimated, approved, rejected).

The log does two jobs. First, it forces every request through the same triage: is this in scope, a **change order candidate**, or a "no, but here's why" conversation — instead of letting it get answered informally and forgotten. Second, it becomes your evidence trail. When a client says six months in "we never agreed to pay extra for that," you're not relying on memory — you point to the log entry, the date, and the follow-up email confirming it.

{: .important }
> A verbal "yes, we can do that" in a meeting is not an approved change — it isn't real until it's logged and confirmed in writing, even if it's just a one-line email reply.

Practically, close the loop fast: log the request within a day, send a one-paragraph written recap to the client the same week ("confirming we discussed X, estimated impact is Y, let us know if you'd like to proceed"), and only mark it approved once someone with authority replies in writing. That habit is what turns "he said, she said" into a clean, defensible record.
