# SOW – The Most Dangerous Document
{: .no_toc }

*Read duration: 1:45*

The **Statement of Work (SOW)** earns its reputation as the most dangerous document in the pre-sales toolkit because it's the one place where vague language turns directly into lost margin, missed deadlines, and damaged trust. The NDA and MSA are largely boilerplate you can reuse across clients; the SOW is bespoke every time, written under deal pressure, and often the last thing finalized before a project kicks off — exactly the conditions under which corners get cut.

What goes wrong is almost always the same pattern: scope is described in outcome language ("modernize the reporting platform") instead of deliverable language, acceptance criteria are left implicit, and assumptions that were spoken out loud in a discovery call never make it onto the page. Six weeks into delivery, the client believes "modernize the reporting platform" included migrating their entire historical dataset; your delivery team believed it meant the ten dashboards discussed in the demo. Both sides read the same sentence and walked away with different projects.

A well-written SOW closes that gap with specificity. Compare a dangerous clause to a defensible one:

- **Dangerous:** *"Vendor will provide analytics dashboards to support Client's reporting needs."*
- **Defensible:** *"Vendor will deliver five (5) dashboards — Sales Performance, Inventory Aging, Regional Revenue, Customer Churn, and Support SLA — built on Client's existing data warehouse, with up to two (2) rounds of revisions per dashboard. Historical data migration is out of scope and will be quoted separately if requested."*

The second version does three things the first doesn't: it names a countable deliverable, it states what's explicitly excluded, and it caps the revision cycle so "two rounds of feedback" can't become ten.

Every solid SOW needs, at minimum: a deliverables list, acceptance criteria (how the client will sign off that each deliverable is "done"), a timeline with milestones, pricing tied to those milestones, assumptions and exclusions, and named points of contact on both sides.

{: .important }
> If it isn't written into the SOW, it isn't in scope — a client saying "but we discussed this on the call" carries no contractual weight, which is exactly why every material discovery finding needs to land in the document itself.
