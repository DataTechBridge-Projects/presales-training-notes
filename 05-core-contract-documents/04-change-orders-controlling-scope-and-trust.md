# Change Orders – Controlling Scope & Trust
{: .no_toc }

*Read duration: 1:51*

No SOW survives contact with a real project unchanged — requirements shift, the client's priorities evolve, or discovery simply missed something. The **change order** is the mechanism that lets scope move without either side losing control of budget, timeline, or trust. It's a short, formal amendment to the SOW: it names the specific change requested, its impact on deliverables and timeline, its price, and requires sign-off from both parties before work begins.

The reason change orders matter so much in pre-sales is that they're where the discipline you built into the SOW either pays off or collapses. If your SOW clearly excluded historical data migration, a client request to add it is an easy, uncontroversial change order — you already agreed it was out of scope, so pricing the addition is straightforward and doesn't feel like a surprise to anyone. If your SOW was vague, the same request becomes an argument about whether it was "already included," and by the time you're negotiating a change order under that kind of tension, you've usually already burned some trust.

A workable change order process has three parts: a **request** (client or delivery team identifies a needed change), an **impact assessment** (what it adds to cost and timeline, ideally turned around within a few business days), and **written approval** before any work starts. The single most common failure is delivery teams doing the extra work first and raising the change order after, on the assumption the client will obviously agree to pay for it — this is called **scope creep**, and it's how profitable projects quietly become break-even ones.

{: .important }
> Never let delivery start on a change before it's signed — informal approval over email or a verbal "yes, go ahead" is not the same as an executed change order, and it's the gap where scope creep gets its foothold.

Framed well to a client, change orders aren't a defensive legal maneuver — they're proof that the relationship has a clear, fair process for handling the unexpected, which is itself a selling point in a competitive deal.
