# Templates & Pre-Sales Toolkit
{: .no_toc }

*Read duration: 2:44*

Every experienced pre-sales rep ends up building the same thing eventually: a personal library of reusable assets that turns a blank page into a starting draft. The difference between someone who treats every opportunity as a fresh essay and someone who treats it as fill-in-the-blank is often the gap between a two-day turnaround and a two-week one — and in a competitive deal, that speed is itself a competitive advantage.

A solid **pre-sales toolkit** covers a handful of recurring document types. A **discovery call template** with your standard question set (mapped to a framework like Pain → Impact → Value → Fit) keeps calls consistent even when you're rushed. A **SOW skeleton** with your standard sections — scope, deliverables, assumptions, exclusions, acceptance criteria — means you're editing, not authoring, under deadline pressure. An **estimation model**, even a simple spreadsheet with historical actuals by work type, keeps your numbers grounded instead of gut-feel. And an **RFP answer library** — a searchable set of previously-approved responses to common questions ("describe your security posture," "how do you handle change requests") — is the single highest-leverage asset in the kit, since the same fifteen questions show up in most RFPs with minor rewording.

Beyond documents, the toolkit includes objection-handling scripts (short, honest answers to "you're more expensive than X" or "why not build this in-house"), a competitive battlecard summarizing how you differ from the two or three vendors you run into most often, and a checklist for handoff to delivery so nothing verbally promised in the sales cycle gets lost before the SOW is signed.

{: .key-term }
> A **toolkit** in this sense isn't a shared drive full of old proposals — it's a curated, maintained set of templates you trust enough to reuse without re-checking every line.

The trap to avoid is letting templates make you lazy. A reused answer that isn't tailored to this client's actual wording reads as generic, and sharp evaluators notice. Treat every template as a 70% draft: it saves you from starting cold, but the final 30% — the client's specific language, their specific pain point, their specific numbers — still has to be written fresh every time.
