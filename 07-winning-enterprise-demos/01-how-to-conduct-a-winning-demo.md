# How to Conduct a Winning Demo
{: .no_toc }

*Read duration: 3:48*

An enterprise demo is not a product tour — it's the moment where discovery either proves out or falls apart in front of the room. Everything you learned in discovery about the client's **Pain, Impact, Value, and Fit** has to show up on screen, or the buying committee will quietly conclude you weren't listening. A winning demo is built in three phases: preparation and customization, delivery, and the close.

## Preparation and customization

Preparation starts weeks before the call, not the night before. Pull your discovery notes and identify the two or three pains that came up most consistently across stakeholders — those become your demo's spine. Generic, out-of-the-box demos read as lazy to enterprise buyers who are evaluating multiple vendors; they've seen the canned version before, often from your competitor. Instead, load the demo environment with the client's own vocabulary: use their product names, their org structure, their sample data format (anonymized if needed). If the client mentioned their claims-processing backlog in discovery, your demo should open on a claims queue, not a generic dashboard.

Customization also means knowing your audience in the room. A demo to a technical evaluator needs to show configuration depth and integration points; the same demo to a VP needs to lead with business outcomes and skip the API details entirely. When you expect a mixed audience — common in enterprise deals — build a flexible path through the demo so you can go deep or stay high-level depending on who's asking questions.

{: .important }
> Never demo a feature you haven't personally tested in the last 24 hours — enterprise buyers remember a broken demo far longer than they remember a good one.

## Delivery

Open by restating the business problem in the client's own words before you touch the keyboard — this confirms you understood discovery and sets the frame for everything that follows. Structure the walkthrough as a narrative: "here's the problem today, here's what changes, here's the outcome," rather than a feature-by-feature tour. Narrate as you click so non-technical stakeholders can follow, and pause after each major moment to check the room rather than plowing through a script. If a stakeholder interrupts with a question, answer it — a demo that ignores real-time engagement to protect a rehearsed flow loses credibility fast.

Keep pacing tight. At under four minutes of planned content, every screen you show needs to earn its place; cut anything that doesn't map back to one of the pains you identified in preparation. Have a backup plan — screenshots or a recorded segment — for any live component that depends on network access or a third-party integration.

## Closing

Don't let the demo end on a feature. Close by explicitly connecting what you just showed back to the client's stated goals, then propose a concrete next step — a technical deep-dive, a pilot scope, or a follow-up with a specific stakeholder who wasn't in the room. Ask directly what's needed to move forward, and get a date on the calendar before you hang up. A demo that ends with "let us know what you think" rarely moves a deal; one that ends with a scheduled next step does.
