# When Customers Don’t Issue RFx
{: .no_toc }

*Read duration: 2:39*

Most deals you'll actually work don't start with a formal document at all. A champion emails "can you send us a proposal for something like this," a stakeholder describes a problem on a discovery call and asks what it would cost, or a partner introduces you to a prospect who has budget but no procurement process in place. There's no RFI, RFQ, or RFP — just an informal, often vague ask — and the temptation is to treat that as a green light to fire off a generic deck. That's a mistake: the absence of a formal process means *you* have to build the structure the customer hasn't provided, or the deal drifts without a clear path to close.

The first move is to run a lightweight discovery pass even when nobody asked for one — confirm the problem, the budget owner, the timeline, and who else needs to sign off, because an informal request often hides an unstated decision process that only surfaces once you're deep into scoping. Ask directly: "Is there a formal RFP process here, or are we working informally?" Buyers will usually tell you straight, and the answer changes how much rigor your response needs.

The second move is to **impose structure yourself**. Even without a customer-issued template, you can propose one: send a short scoping questionnaire, or frame your response as "Here's what I understood from our call — confirm this is accurate before I put together a formal proposal." This does two things — it surfaces gaps in your own understanding before you commit numbers to paper, and it signals professionalism that separates you from competitors who just email a generic PDF.

{: .important }
> An informal ask is not license to skip discovery — it means you own the job of creating the structure a formal RFx would have handed you for free.

The third move is pacing: informal deals can stall indefinitely because there's no submission deadline forcing momentum. Set your own — "I'll have a draft proposal to you by Friday, and we can walk through it together" — to manufacture the urgency a formal RFx process would have supplied on its own. Treat every informal opportunity as a chance to shape the deal on your terms, rather than reacting to whatever the customer happens to send you.
