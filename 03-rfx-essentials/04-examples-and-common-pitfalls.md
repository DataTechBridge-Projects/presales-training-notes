# Examples & Common Pitfalls
{: .no_toc }

*Read duration: 3:08*

The gap between a winning RFP response and a losing one is rarely about capability — most shortlisted vendors could technically do the work. It's about execution on the document itself. Take a common scenario: a mid-market customer issues an RFP for a data-migration project with a requirement that reads "Vendor must provide a detailed rollback plan in the event of migration failure." A weak response answers with one line — "Rollback procedures will be documented during planning" — which is technically true but tells the evaluator nothing and reads as if the vendor hasn't actually thought about failure scenarios. A strong response spells out the actual mechanism: a point-in-time snapshot taken before cutover, a defined rollback window, named roles responsible for the go/no-go call, and a rehearsal of the rollback in a staging environment before the live event. Same underlying capability, completely different evaluator confidence.

**Pitfall one: answering the RFP you wish you'd received instead of the one you got.** Reps often lean on a favorite past project or a killer feature and force it into every answer, regardless of whether the customer asked for it. Evaluators notice when an answer doesn't match the question, and it reads as either not listening or padding around a gap.

**Pitfall two: generic boilerplate that could apply to any customer.** A paragraph about your company's "20 years of industry-leading excellence" that never mentions the customer's actual named problem gets skimmed and discounted — every competitor's boilerplate reads the same to a tired evaluator on their fifteenth submission of the week.

**Pitfall three: pricing presented without context.** A number with no breakdown of what's included, what's excluded, and what assumptions it rests on invites the evaluator to assume the worst about hidden costs, even when your price is actually competitive.

**Pitfall four: missing the compliance formalities.** Signature pages, required certifications, exact file-naming conventions, and submission deadlines sound trivial, but a technically excellent response that gets disqualified on a missed formality never even reaches a scoring committee — procurement teams enforce these rules literally, with no exceptions for a great proposal.

{: .important }
> A brilliant response that misses a mandatory formality — a missing signature, a late submission, the wrong file format — can be disqualified before a single evaluator reads a word of the content.

The pattern across every pitfall is the same: RFx evaluators are pattern-matching for specificity and compliance under time pressure, and the response that makes their job easiest — direct answers, real numbers, no wasted words — consistently outperforms the response that's merely the most impressive to read.
