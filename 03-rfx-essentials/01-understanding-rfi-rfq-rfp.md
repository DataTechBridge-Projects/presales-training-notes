# Understanding RFI, RFQ, RFP
{: .no_toc }

*Read duration: 2:57*

Procurement teams use three distinct instruments to structure how they buy, and confusing them will cost you time, credibility, or both. A **Request for Information (RFI)** is a fact-finding exercise — the customer doesn't yet know what solutions exist or who the credible vendors are, so they're asking broad questions about your capabilities, references, and approach with no pricing commitment attached. A **Request for Quote (RFQ)** flips that: the customer already knows exactly what they want (a defined scope, a specific product, a known quantity of work) and is asking you to price it, usually to compare against competitors on cost alone. A **Request for Proposal (RFP)** sits between the two in a different dimension entirely — the customer has a problem or outcome they want solved, they're asking multiple vendors to propose *how* they'd solve it, and price is one input among several (approach, team, references, timeline) that gets scored.

The strategic mistake pre-sales reps make is treating all three the same way — answering an RFQ with a long capabilities narrative, or answering an RFI with a firm price, both of which read as tone-deaf to the buyer's actual stage in their process.

| Type | Purpose | When issued | Expected response |
|---|---|---|---|
| RFI | Gather information, map the vendor landscape | Early — before scope is defined | Capability overview, references, no pricing |
| RFQ | Get comparable pricing on a defined scope | Late — scope is already fixed | Itemized quote, minimal narrative |
| RFP | Evaluate approach, fit, and price together | Mid-to-late — problem is defined, solution isn't | Full proposal: approach, team, timeline, price |

An RFI response should read like a credibility pitch — short, sharp, and focused on relevant proof points, since your goal is simply to make the shortlist for whatever comes next. An RFQ response should be lean and precise, because burying a tight, comparable price inside marketing prose actually hurts you — evaluators are scanning for a number, not a story. An RFP response is where you invest the most: it's the one document where a well-argued approach can beat a lower price, because the buyer is explicitly scoring more than cost.

{: .important }
> Don't confuse RFI with RFQ: responding to an information request with a locked-in price signals you didn't read the document, and it can anchor you to a number before you understand the real scope.

Knowing which of the three you've received — sometimes buyers mislabel their own documents — is the first strategic read you make before writing a single word of response.
