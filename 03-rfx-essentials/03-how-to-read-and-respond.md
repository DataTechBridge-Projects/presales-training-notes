# How to Read and Respond
{: .no_toc }

*Read duration: 3:05*

A client document rarely states its real priorities in plain language — you have to read between the lines to find them. Word count is one signal: if a 20-page RFP spends three pages on data security and two sentences on implementation timeline, security is what's actually driving the decision, regardless of how the executive summary is worded. Requirement phrasing is another: "must support" language is a hard gate that disqualifies you if you can't meet it, while "should support" or "nice to have" language is a scored preference, not a wall. Confusing the two leads to two failure modes — either you disqualify yourself by hedging on a soft requirement, or you overpromise on a hard one and get caught in due diligence.

Pay close attention to language that's been copied from an incumbent vendor's product literature — it's a strong signal the customer already has a preferred vendor and the RFP is a compliance exercise to satisfy procurement policy rather than a genuinely open competition. You can often spot this from oddly specific technical requirements that only one product on the market actually meets. That doesn't mean you shouldn't respond — sometimes an incumbent-favored process is still winnable if their delivery has soured the relationship — but it changes your investment calculus and how hard you push on price.

**Crafting the response** starts with mirroring the customer's own structure and terminology. If their document asks for information in a specific numbered format, respond in that exact numbered format — evaluators scoring dozens of submissions reward reviewers who make their job easy and penalize creative reformatting that forces them to hunt for answers. Lead every section with the outcome the customer cares about, not a feature list; "Our platform reduces onboarding time from six weeks to ten days" beats "Our platform includes an onboarding module" every time, because it answers the unstated question — *why should I care* — that sits behind every requirement.

{: .important }
> Never leave a requirement unanswered or vague to avoid admitting a gap — an evaluator reads silence as a bigger red flag than an honest "not currently supported, workaround available," and unaddressed requirements often get scored as an automatic zero.

Finally, use the compliance matrix or requirements table the customer provides (or build one if they didn't) to confirm, line by line, that every single requirement has a direct, findable answer somewhere in your response — evaluators are working through a checklist, and a strong narrative buried in prose with no clear line back to their requirement number effectively doesn't exist to them.
