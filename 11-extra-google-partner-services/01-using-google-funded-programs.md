# Using Google-Funded Programs
{: .no_toc }

*Read duration: 1:50*

Google's partner-funded programs — the **Partner Sales Fund (PSF)** and **Partner Solutions Organization (PSO)** engagements covered in the next lecture — exist because Google would rather subsidize a well-run migration or proof-of-concept than lose the deal to a lower price point or a slower timeline from a competing cloud. As a pre-sales lead, your job is to know these programs exist before the proposal goes out, not scramble to find funding after the client has already pushed back on price.

Applying starts inside the Partner Advantage console, or through your Google partner engineering or partner development manager (PDM) contact, with a funding request tied to a specific opportunity: workload description, target GCP services, estimated consumption, and a rough timeline. Google wants to see a **real, qualified pipeline opportunity**, not a hypothetical use case — you'll typically need the opportunity registered, and at a minimum stage in Google's own CRM view, before a request is even considered.

What gets approved skews toward activities that reduce Google's own risk in the deal: architecture workshops, proof-of-concept builds, migration assessments, and hands-on enablement for the client's technical team. Pure discount requests or general marketing spend rarely qualify — the funding attaches to *delivery work* that visibly moves a specific opportunity forward, not to padding your margin.

{: .important }
> Funded programs cover services and enablement, not runway to pad your margin — treat them as deal accelerants, not discount substitutes, or your request will get bounced back.

When you position this with a client, frame it as validation, not charity: "Google is co-investing in de-risking this migration because they believe in the approach" lands far better than mentioning a fee waiver. Raise it early in the proposal conversation, once discovery has confirmed a genuine GCP fit, so it reads as a deliberate part of the deal structure rather than a last-minute concession pulled out when the client balks at price.
