# Roles in Pre-Sales (AE, Architect, Legal, Delivery)
{: .no_toc }

*Read duration: 2:56*

A deal moves through the pre-sales lifecycle because several people are each doing a different job on it at the same time, not because one person owns it end to end. Knowing who owns what — and, more importantly, who you need to loop in before you commit to something on their behalf — is what keeps a fast-moving deal from turning into a mess of contradictory promises.

The **account executive (AE)** owns the relationship and the commercial outcome. They're driving the timeline, managing stakeholder politics, and ultimately deciding whether a deal is worth pursuing — but they usually aren't the one who can say whether a solution is technically feasible or what it will cost to build. That's where the **solutions architect** comes in: they translate discovery findings into a concrete technical approach, own the estimate that backs up the price, and are the person who has to stand behind the SOW's scope language once it's signed. When an AE promises a capability in a client meeting without checking with the architect first, the architect is the one who inherits that promise during delivery.

**Legal** (or a commercial/contracts function, even if it's one person wearing multiple hats) reviews and negotiates the NDA, MSA, and SOW language — protecting the company from liability, IP disputes, and vague commitments that create legal exposure later. Legal isn't there to slow the deal down; they're there because a contract with ambiguous termination or IP clauses causes far more damage than a week's delay in review.

Finally, **delivery** — the project or engagement lead who will actually execute the work once the deal closes — should never be a stranger to the deal at signature. Delivery needs to sanity-check the estimate, the assumptions, and the scope boundaries *before* the client signs, not discover them for the first time in a kickoff call. A deal that "wins" by keeping delivery in the dark just moves the conflict from the negotiation table to the project's first status report.

{: .important }
> Never let one role make a commitment that belongs to another — an AE promising scope, an architect quoting price without delivery sign-off, or legal being looped in after terms are already verbally agreed all create the same outcome: a deal the company can't actually honor.

These four roles collaborate continuously across the lifecycle stages covered earlier — qualification and discovery lean on the AE and architect together, scoping and estimation need architect and delivery alignment, and contracting needs legal and the AE working in lockstep. The tighter that collaboration, the fewer surprises show up after the ink is dry.
